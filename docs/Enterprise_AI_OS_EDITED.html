How Your Enterprise AI Operating System Actually Works

Not a list of buzzwords. A single mental model you can hold in your head, built piece by piece — including how the system protects confidential company data, separates companies, and proves that every answer and action was authorized.

01 · Orientation

The Big Picture

The problem you're solving: inside every mid-to-large company, knowledge is scattered and locked away. HR policy is a PDF nobody reads. Contracts sit in a shared drive. The sales numbers live in a database only the analytics team can query. The codebase only makes sense to whoever wrote it. Meeting notes evaporate into a Slack channel. Every employee who needs an answer has to know where to look and who to ask — and most don't, so they either guess, wait, or bother someone else.

The security problem you're solving at the same time: connecting an AI to all of that knowledge can create a new risk. A normal employee must not retrieve payroll records, one department must not see another department's confidential files, and a user from Company A must never receive data belonging to Company B. Access control therefore cannot be a login screen added at the end — it must be part of retrieval, SQL queries, agent tool calls, caching, file access, and audit logging.

An Enterprise AI Operating System is the controlled layer that sits on top of scattered company knowledge and lets an employee just ask, in plain language, and get a correct, sourced, useful answer — or better, get the AI to actually go do the task — but only inside the exact data and action scope that employee is authorized to use.

Why would a company actually pay for this?

  • Time. Every "how many vacation days do I get" question that used to take a Slack message and a 20-minute wait now takes 3 seconds.
  • Consistency. The AI always answers from the current policy document, not from what someone remembers from 2019.
  • Leverage. One data engineer's SQL skill becomes queryable by every non-technical employee in the company.
  • Institutional memory. When someone leaves the company, their knowledge doesn't leave with them — it's already indexed.

Why is this "more than a chatbot"?

A chatbot answers questions using only what it was trained on, and it can't take action. Your system does three things a chatbot cannot:

  • It retrieves live, private, constantly-changing company data before answering (RAG) — so it isn't guessing from stale training data.
  • It decides what steps a request actually requires and calls real tools to do them (agents, tool calling, planning) — a chatbot only talks; your system acts.
  • It checks itself before responding (hallucination checking, evaluation) — a chatbot has no idea whether it just made something up.

Why is it "an AI system" and not just "software that calls an API"?

Because the intelligence isn't just "send text to GPT and print the reply." There's a pipeline of AI decisions happening: deciding what kind of question this is, deciding which data source answers it, deciding whether a tool needs to run, deciding whether the answer is trustworthy enough to show the user. Each of those is a small AI-driven decision. Stack enough of them together, orchestrated, and you get a system that behaves less like "autocomplete with a search bar" and more like a junior analyst who knows exactly where to look and what to do — that composition is what makes it a system, not a wrapper.

02 · Walkthrough

The System As A Story

A company uploads its HR policies, contracts, sales database, GitHub repo, meeting notes, and support docs. Now four employees ask four very different questions. Watch what actually happens behind the scenes for each one — nothing skipped.

Employee asks"How many vacation days do I get?"
Type of question
Simple factual lookup against a static document. This is a textbook RAG case — no tools needed, no calculation needed.
What runs first
The Planner/Orchestrator classifies intent: "informational, single-source, no action required." It routes straight to retrieval — it does not wake up any agent or tool.
Which component works
The Embedding Model turns the question into a vector. The Vector Database (Qdrant) searches the HR policy chunks for the closest semantic matches — not keyword matches, meaning-matches, so "vacation days" also finds a section titled "Annual Leave Entitlement."
What data it uses
Only the HR policy document chunks flagged for that employee's country/contract type (metadata filtering matters here — a US policy shouldn't answer an Egypt-based employee).
What the AI is "thinking"
The LLM receives the question plus the 3–5 retrieved policy chunks as context, and is instructed: "answer only from the provided context, cite the source section, say 'I don't know' if it isn't there."
Verification
The Hallucination Checker confirms every sentence in the draft answer is actually traceable to a retrieved chunk — not invented.
Final answer shown to user"You're entitled to 21 paid vacation days per year, per the 2026 HR Policy, Section 4.2 — accrued monthly after your probation period."
Employee asks"Generate last month's sales report."
Type of question
Not a document-lookup question at all — it requires running a live query against a structured database and formatting the result. This is a tool-calling / agent case, not RAG.
What runs first
The Planner recognizes this needs structured data, not unstructured text, and selects the SQL Tool / Database Agent instead of the vector retriever.
Which component works
The agent generates a SQL query (e.g. aggregate revenue by product/region for the previous calendar month) against PostgreSQL, executes it through a controlled, read-only connection, and gets back rows.
Why it works this way
You never let an LLM "guess" sales numbers — numbers must come from a real query. The LLM's job here is translating natural language into SQL and then translating the SQL result back into a readable narrative, not inventing figures.
What the AI is "thinking"
"This is a data-retrieval + summarization task. Step 1: write query. Step 2: execute. Step 3: check result isn't empty/malformed. Step 4: summarize trends in plain language."
Verification
The Evaluation Module checks the SQL didn't touch a forbidden table, and that the totals mentioned in the text actually match the numbers returned by the query (no rounding hallucinations).
Final answer shown to user"June revenue was $482,300, up 6% vs May. EMEA led growth (+14%), driven mostly by the Enterprise tier. [Attached: sales_report_june.csv]"
Employee asks"Explain the authentication module."
Type of question
A code-comprehension question against the GitHub repository — RAG again, but over code, which is chunked and embedded differently than prose (by function/class, respecting syntax boundaries).
What runs first
The Planner detects "code" intent (keywords, repo context, or explicit tool selection) and routes to the code-aware retriever rather than the general document retriever.
Which component works
The Vector DB searches code embeddings for files/functions related to "authentication" — likely surfacing auth_service.py, jwt_handler.py, and related tests.
What data it uses
Retrieved source files plus any docstrings/README sections describing the auth flow, and possibly recent commit messages for context on why something changed.
What the AI is "thinking"
The LLM reads the retrieved code as context and is prompted to explain control flow in plain English: what triggers the module, what it validates, what it returns, what it depends on.
Verification
The Hallucination Checker confirms function names, parameters, and behavior mentioned in the explanation actually exist in the retrieved source — it's very easy for an LLM to invent a plausible-sounding function that isn't real.
Final answer shown to user"Authentication is handled in auth_service.py. It issues a signed JWT on login (jwt_handler.py), validated on every request by verify_token() middleware, which checks expiry and role claims before allowing access."
Employee asks"Compare these two contracts."
Type of question
Multi-document reasoning: needs both contracts retrieved in full relevant context and then compared clause-by-clause — a harder RAG case that may involve a short multi-step plan.
What runs first
The Planner recognizes two named documents are involved and issues two retrieval calls, one per contract, rather than one generic search.
Which component works
The Vector DB returns the most relevant clauses from each contract (payment terms, termination, liability, etc.), and the Orchestrator aligns matching clause types between the two sets before handing them to the LLM.
What the AI is "thinking"
"For each clause category present in both documents, state what each contract says, then state the material difference." This is structured comparison, not free-form chat.
Verification
The Hallucination Checker confirms every quoted clause or number is traceable to the specific contract cited — critical here, since contract mistakes have real legal consequences.
Final answer shown to user"Termination: Contract A requires 30 days' notice; Contract B requires 90. Liability cap: A caps at $50K, B is uncapped. Payment terms are otherwise identical (net-30)."
03 · Anatomy

Every Component, Explained

For each piece: what it is, why you need it, what breaks if you remove it, and how it talks to the rest of the system.

01Frontend
What it is
The web app the employee actually sees and types into — chat interface, file upload, dashboards, report viewers.
Why you need it
It's the only part of the system a human directly touches. Everything else exists to answer what the frontend asks on the user's behalf.
If removed
The system still "works" but only via raw API calls — unusable by a non-technical employee, which defeats the whole point.
How it communicates
Sends HTTP requests (usually with streaming for token-by-token responses) to the Backend/FastAPI layer, holds the auth token, renders whatever comes back (text, tables, charts, file links).
02Backend (FastAPI)
What it is
The central Python service that exposes REST/streaming endpoints, handles auth, and coordinates every other component. FastAPI specifically because it's async-native (important — you'll be waiting on LLM calls, DB calls, and vector searches concurrently) and gives you automatic request validation and docs.
Why you need it
The frontend should never talk directly to the database, the vector store, or the LLM provider — that's a security and maintainability disaster. The backend is the single gatekeeper.
If removed
No system. This is the skeleton everything else hangs on.
How it communicates
Receives requests from the Frontend; calls the Orchestrator/Planner; queries PostgreSQL, Qdrant, and Redis; writes/reads Object Storage; calls the LLM provider; returns the final structured response.
03PostgreSQL
What it is
Your relational database of record: companies/tenants, users, departments, roles, permissions, resource ACLs, chat history, uploaded document metadata, HR/sales/business data, and allowed/denied audit logs.
Why you need it
Anything that needs strict structure, relationships, and transactional integrity (user A cannot see document B they don't have permission for) belongs here — not in a vector store.
If removed
No reliable user accounts, no permission model, no structured business data to query for the "sales report" scenario — the system becomes unsafe and stateless.
How it communicates
Queried directly by the Backend (via an ORM like SQLAlchemy) and by the SQL Tool that agents call during tool-calling for structured-data questions.
04Vector Database (Qdrant)
What it is
A database built to store embeddings (numeric fingerprints of meaning) and search by similarity of meaning rather than exact keyword match.
Why you need it
This is the engine behind RAG. Without it, the LLM has no way to find "the relevant paragraph out of 10,000 pages" — it would have to read everything, every time, which is slow, expensive, and eventually impossible (context limits).
If removed
No RAG at all. The system falls back to a plain chatbot that only knows generic training data — none of the company's private documents.
How it communicates
The Backend/Retriever sends it a query vector plus mandatory payload filters derived from the user's trusted access context. Qdrant returns only the top-K similar chunks that pass tenant, role, department, country, owner, and classification rules.
05Redis
What it is
An in-memory key-value store, used for caching and short-lived state — session data, rate limiting, caching recent query results, queuing background jobs.
Why you need it
LLM calls and vector searches are relatively slow and costly. If ten employees ask the same HR question in one morning, you don't want to re-run the whole pipeline ten times.
If removed
The system still functions, just slower and more expensive under load — every request pays full price, and there's no fast layer for session/job state.
How it communicates
Sits beside the Backend; the Backend checks Redis before running an expensive pipeline step, and writes results back into Redis with an expiry.
06Object Storage
What it is
Storage for raw files themselves — the original PDFs, docx contracts, spreadsheets — separate from the database (which stores metadata, not the files).
Why you need it
Databases are bad at storing large binary blobs efficiently. Object storage (S3-compatible, e.g. MinIO for a self-hosted setup) is built exactly for this, and lets you regenerate embeddings later without re-uploading.
If removed
You'd have nowhere durable to keep the original uploaded documents — only their chopped-up, embedded fragments, which is useless if you ever need to show or re-process the original file.
How it communicates
Backend writes uploaded files here on ingestion; the Ingestion Pipeline reads from here to chunk and embed; links are handed back to the Frontend so users can view/download originals.
07LLM (Large Language Model)
What it is
The reasoning and language-generation engine — the part that actually reads context and produces the natural-language answer, plan, or code.
Why you need it
Every other component gathers or verifies information; the LLM is what turns "here's a pile of relevant data" into "here's a clear, human-readable answer."
If removed
You'd have a very good search engine, but no natural-language understanding or generation — no chat, no summarization, no explanation.
How it communicates
Called by the Orchestrator/Planner and by individual agents, via API, with a prompt built from the user's question plus retrieved context plus tool results.
08Embedding Model
What it is
A smaller, specialized model whose only job is turning text (or code, or images) into a vector — a list of numbers representing meaning, positioned so similar meanings sit close together in that numeric space.
Why you need it
It's the translator that makes semantic search in the Vector Database possible at all. Without embeddings, "search" degenerates back into keyword matching.
If removed
No semantic search, no RAG — you're back to keyword/regex search over documents, which misses paraphrased questions entirely.
How it communicates
Runs during ingestion (embedding every document chunk once, stored in Qdrant) and at query time (embedding the user's question so it can be compared).
09RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)
What it is
Not a single tool — it's a technique: retrieve the most relevant real data first, then hand it to the LLM as context before asking it to answer.
Why you need it
It's how a general-purpose LLM (trained on public internet text) becomes an expert on this specific company's private, current documents — without retraining the model.
If removed
The LLM answers purely from what it memorized during training — it knows nothing about this company's HR policy, contracts, or codebase, and will confidently guess wrong.
How it communicates
Sits conceptually between the Vector DB (retrieval half) and the LLM (generation half); the Orchestrator wires these two steps together for every knowledge question.
10Fine-Tuning
What it is
Actually adjusting a model's internal weights on a custom dataset, so its default behavior/style/domain knowledge shifts — different from RAG, which only changes what context the model sees, not the model itself.
Why you need it
Useful for narrow, repeated tasks: teaching a small model your company's tone of voice, a strict output format, or a specialized classification (e.g. routing support tickets) — cheaper to run at scale than always relying on a giant general model.
If removed
You can still ship the whole product using only pre-trained models plus RAG — fine-tuning is an optimization, not a hard requirement for an MVP.
How it communicates
Happens offline/asynchronously (a training job, not a live request); the resulting fine-tuned model is then deployed and called just like any other model by the Orchestrator.
11Planner Agent
What it is
The component that looks at an incoming request and decides what needs to happen — one retrieval step? A database query? Several tools in sequence?
Why you need it
Not every question is a simple lookup. Some need multiple steps in a specific order (e.g. "compare two contracts" = retrieve A, retrieve B, then compare). The Planner is what makes the system capable of multi-step reasoning instead of one-shot replies.
If removed
Every request gets treated identically — you lose the ability to handle anything beyond a single flat RAG lookup.
How it communicates
Sits right after the Backend receives a request; outputs a plan/route that's handed to the Retriever and/or specific Tools; often implemented as a graph of steps (e.g. with LangGraph).
12Tool Calling
What it is
The mechanism that lets an LLM say "I need to run this specific function with these arguments" instead of just generating text — e.g. run_sql_query(query), search_documents(query), send_email(to, body).
Why you need it
This is what turns the system from "answers questions" into "does things." Without it, the LLM can only talk about the sales report; with it, it can actually go generate one.
If removed
No agents, no automation — the system reverts to a read-only Q&A chatbot with RAG, nothing more.
How it communicates
The LLM outputs a structured "call this function with these arguments" instruction; the Orchestrator executes the real function (hitting PostgreSQL, an API, a file system) and feeds the result back to the LLM for the final answer.
13AI Orchestrator
What it is
The conductor. It owns the full request lifecycle: receive question → consult Planner → run Retriever and/or Tools → assemble context → call the LLM → run verification → return result.
Why you need it
Without a single coordinating layer, every component would need to know about every other component — a tangled mess. The Orchestrator is the one place that knows the whole flow.
If removed
Every piece exists but nothing connects them — you'd need to manually wire retrieval, tools, and generation together inside the Backend itself, which quickly becomes unmaintainable.
How it communicates
Called by the Backend for every AI request; it in turn calls the Planner, Retriever, Tools, LLM, and Verifier, in whatever order the Planner decided.
14Authorization Policy Engine
What it is
The deterministic security layer that combines tenant membership, roles, permissions, department/country/ownership attributes, document classification, and exceptional resource ACLs.
Why you need it
Authentication only proves identity. This layer decides whether that identity may retrieve a chunk, read a row, download a file, call an agent tool, view a cached answer, or approve an action.
If removed
The AI may answer correctly but reveal the answer to the wrong person — a more serious enterprise failure than an ordinary hallucination.
How it communicates
The Backend builds a trusted access context and calls the engine before execution. The resulting scope becomes PostgreSQL filters/RLS context, Qdrant payload filters, Object Storage paths, Redis cache namespaces, and tool permissions. Denials are written to the audit log.
15Hallucination Checker
What it is
A verification step (often a second, cheaper LLM call, or a rule-based check) that compares the draft answer against the retrieved source material and flags claims that aren't actually supported.
Why you need it
LLMs generate fluent, confident-sounding text even when wrong. In an enterprise setting (contracts, HR policy, financial numbers), an unverified hallucination is a real liability, not just an annoyance.
If removed
The system will occasionally state confident, plausible-sounding facts that are simply false, with no safety net catching it before the user sees it.
How it communicates
Runs after the LLM produces a draft answer and before the Orchestrator returns it to the Backend; if it fails the check, the Orchestrator can retry retrieval, ask the LLM to revise, or return a cautious "I'm not confident" response.
16Evaluation Module
What it is
The offline/ongoing measurement system: how accurate are answers, how relevant is retrieval, how often do agents pick the right tool, tracked over time with test question sets and metrics.
Why you need it
Without measurement, you're guessing whether changes (new prompt, new model, new chunking strategy) made the system better or worse. This is also exactly what a graduation project committee will want to see as evidence the system genuinely works.
If removed
You have no way to prove quality objectively, no regression detection when you change something, and no data-driven way to improve retrieval or prompts.
How it communicates
Runs separately from live user traffic — a scheduled or manually-triggered process that replays a test set through the Orchestrator and scores the outputs, logging results to PostgreSQL/a dashboard.
17Scheduler / Background Worker
What it is
A job runner (e.g. Celery, with Redis as the task queue) that triggers the Orchestrator on a timer or an event — a new file upload, a database row change — instead of waiting for a user to type a message.
Why you need it
It's what turns the system from purely reactive into proactive: the weekly sales summary that's already waiting Monday morning, or the contract that gets auto-scanned for risky clauses the moment it's uploaded, without anyone having to ask.
If removed
The system can still do everything it does today, but only in response to a person typing a question — nothing ever runs on the company's behalf unprompted.
How it communicates
Calls the exact same Orchestrator entry point a live chat request would; the Orchestrator processes it identically either way, so nothing downstream needs to know the difference.
18Human Approval Gate
What it is
A checkpoint the Orchestrator passes through before executing any tool call that can't be undone (sending an email, deleting a record, editing a live document) — it pauses and waits for the user to explicitly confirm.
Why you need it
Tool calling gives the LLM the ability to act, not just talk — and an LLM's plan can be subtly wrong (wrong recipient, wrong record). This gate is the seatbelt: it costs one extra click, and it's what makes giving an AI real-world actions defensible rather than reckless.
If removed
Every action a plan decides on fires immediately with no human check — fine for read-only lookups, genuinely risky for anything that writes or sends.
How it communicates
Sits inside the Orchestrator, right after the Hallucination Checker passes and right before execution; the Backend exposes a small confirm/reject endpoint the Frontend calls, and the Orchestrator only proceeds once that confirmation comes back.

The Multi-Agent Roster

Underneath the single AI Orchestrator sits a set of narrow, specialist agents. The Orchestrator doesn't try to know everything itself — it routes each request to whichever of these actually owns that kind of work, then assembles their outputs into one answer.

19Orchestrator Agent (routes tasks)
What it is
The top-level agent in the multi-agent layer whose only job is reading an incoming request and handing it off to the one specialist agent — or sequence of agents — best suited to handle it.
Why you need it
Without a dedicated router, every specialist agent would need its own logic for deciding "is this mine to handle?" — messy and duplicated. One agent owning routing keeps every specialist narrow and focused.
If removed
Every request would need to guess for itself which specialist(s) apply, or you'd fall back to one giant agent trying to do every job at once — poorly.
How it communicates
Sits right beneath the Planner; receives the classified intent, calls the appropriate specialist agent(s) below, and stitches their individual outputs into one coherent reply.
20Knowledge Agent (RAG + documents)
What it is
The specialist that owns unstructured-document questions — HR policy, wikis, meeting notes — by wrapping the RAG pipeline (Embedding Model → Vector DB → cited chunks).
Why you need it
Document questions need semantic retrieval, not SQL or code parsing. Giving this its own agent keeps document grounding consistent no matter which other agents are also running.
If removed
Document-grounded answers would have to be re-implemented inside every other agent that occasionally needs a policy or doc lookup — duplicated retrieval logic everywhere.
How it communicates
Called by the Orchestrator Agent for knowledge-type intents; embeds the question, queries the Vector DB, and returns the top-K chunks with source citations.
21Database Agent (SQL and analytics)
What it is
The specialist that turns a natural-language question into a safe SQL query against structured data — sales, users, operational tables — and returns the analytics result.
Why you need it
Numbers live in rows and columns, not paragraphs. This needs a different tool (Tool Calling → SQL) than the Knowledge Agent's retrieval, and its own guardrails against destructive queries.
If removed
Structured-data questions ("what were Q3 sales by region?") would have no reliable path to an answer — the LLM would have to guess numbers instead of querying them.
How it communicates
Called by the Orchestrator Agent for analytics-type intents; uses Tool Calling to run read-only SQL against PostgreSQL and hands the result rows back for the final answer.
22Code Agent (GitHub analysis)
What it is
The specialist that reads and reasons about the codebase — explaining functions, spotting likely bugs, summarizing pull requests — by calling the GitHub API and/or a code-specific retriever.
Why you need it
Code has its own structure and context (imports, diffs, commit history) that a general document retriever handles poorly. This gives developer-facing questions a specialist built for that shape of data.
If removed
Code-related questions would fall to the generic Knowledge Agent, which has no real understanding of repos, diffs, or code structure — answers would be shallow or wrong.
How it communicates
Called by the Orchestrator Agent for code-related intents; calls the GitHub API as a tool and may query a code-specific index in the Vector DB before generating its answer.
23Legal Agent (contracts and compliance)
What it is
The specialist that extracts clauses from contracts, flags risky or non-compliant terms, and checks documents against known policy — the formal version of the "Contract Agent / Risk Agent" example used earlier in this doc.
Why you need it
Legal and compliance judgments carry real liability if wrong. A narrow agent with its own careful prompting and checks is safer than trusting a general-purpose answer.
If removed
Contract and compliance review would revert to a generic LLM response with no domain-specific risk-flagging — exactly the kind of confident-but-wrong answer this system is meant to prevent.
How it communicates
Called by the Orchestrator Agent for contract/compliance intents; often pulls supporting context via the Knowledge Agent, and anything it recommends as a binding action passes through the Human Approval Gate.
24Workflow Agent (automation and approvals)
What it is
The specialist that manages multi-step business processes — routing an approval through the right people, tracking where a request sits in a process — rather than just answering a single question.
Why you need it
Some tasks aren't one-shot lookups; they're state machines that span multiple steps and sometimes multiple days. This agent is what tracks and advances that state.
If removed
There'd be no way to model an ongoing, multi-step process — every request would need to be re-explained from scratch each time someone checks in on it.
How it communicates
Works alongside the Human Approval Gate and Scheduler/Background Worker; persists process state in PostgreSQL/Redis and triggers the next step once a condition (approval, timer, event) is met.
25Monitoring Agent (autonomous event detection)
What it is
The specialist that watches for events and anomalies proactively — a new upload, unusual database activity, a failed job — and decides whether they warrant action.
Why you need it
Not everything should wait for a person to ask. This agent is the system's "eyes open" layer, catching things worth flagging before anyone notices on their own.
If removed
The system stays purely reactive — nothing gets caught or escalated unless a human happens to go looking for it first.
How it communicates
Triggered by the Scheduler/Background Worker or event hooks; when it detects something worth acting on, it calls the relevant specialist agent (e.g. handing a newly uploaded contract to the Legal Agent) or alerts a human.
26Report Agent (dashboards, summaries, presentations)
What it is
The specialist that takes whatever other agents produced — SQL rows, retrieved chunks, flagged clauses — and assembles it into a polished, human-facing deliverable: a dashboard, a written summary, or a slide deck.
Why you need it
Raw agent output isn't something a person wants handed to them directly. A dedicated formatting/presentation specialist is what turns "data" into "something you'd actually send to your boss."
If removed
Users would get raw rows, chunks, or bullet points back instead of a coherent, presentable result — technically correct, practically unusable.
How it communicates
Called last in a plan, after the Database, Knowledge, Legal, or other agents return their findings; formats the combined result for whatever the Frontend needs to render (chat, dashboard widget, or exportable file).

The key thing to notice: the LLM is only one box in this chain, and it's deliberately positioned in the middle — never at the start (it doesn't get to decide the plan alone) and never at the end unchecked. Two extra gates now sit between the LLM and the user: the self-correction loop, which sends a failed check back into the pipeline instead of forward to the user, and the human approval gate, which only fires for actions that can't be undone (an email sent, a record changed) — most questions, like this contract comparison, skip it entirely and go straight through.

05 · Engineering View

The Same Journey, As A Software Engineer Sees It

1. Authentication

The Frontend attaches a JWT (issued at login) to every request. The Backend verifies the signature and expiry on every call, and looks up the user's role/permissions in PostgreSQL — this is what later restricts which documents can even be retrieved for that user.

2. Authorization context

After identity is verified, the Backend builds a trusted access context containing company_id, user ID, department, country, roles, permissions, manager relationships, and resource ownership. This context is passed to the Orchestrator and converted into deterministic database, vector-search, storage, and tool filters.

3. API call

The Frontend calls something like POST /api/chat with the message and conversation ID. FastAPI validates the request body against a Pydantic schema before anything else runs — malformed input never reaches business logic.

4. Caching check

The Backend hashes the normalized question (plus user's permission scope) and checks Redis. A hit returns instantly. A miss proceeds to the full pipeline — this alone can cut cost and latency dramatically for common questions like the vacation-days example.

5. Vector search

The Retriever service calls the Embedding Model to vectorize the query, then issues a similarity search against Qdrant, filtered by metadata (document type, department, permission tags) so a user only ever retrieves chunks they're authorized to see.

6. Database operations

For structured questions, an agent generates parameterized SQL (never raw string interpolation — that's an injection risk) and executes it through a restricted, read-only database role.

7. File storage

Original uploaded files stay in Object Storage; PostgreSQL stores only metadata (filename, uploader, permissions, storage path, ingestion status). This separation keeps the database fast and the file layer independently scalable.

8. Response generation & streaming

The Backend streams the LLM's tokens to the Frontend over Server-Sent Events or WebSockets rather than waiting for the full answer — this is what makes the UI feel responsive instead of frozen for several seconds.

9. Logging

Every interaction (question, retrieved sources, final answer, latency, model used) gets written to PostgreSQL — this log is what your Evaluation Module and your graduation-project metrics will be built on.

10. Approval gate (irreversible actions only)

If the plan includes an action that can't be undone — sending an email, writing to a live document, deleting a record — the Backend stops just short of executing it and exposes a small POST /api/actions/{id}/confirm endpoint. The Frontend shows a confirmation prompt; nothing fires until the user taps confirm. Read-only actions (a SQL SELECT, a document retrieval) never touch this gate — it only exists for the small subset of tool calls that write.

11. Scheduled execution

Separately from any user request, a Celery worker (backed by Redis as the task queue) wakes up on a cron schedule or a storage event (e.g. a new file landing in Object Storage) and calls the exact same Orchestrator entry point a user's message would — the Orchestrator has no idea whether it was triggered by a person typing or a timer firing, which is exactly what keeps this simple to build.

06 · Security & Data Strategy

Access Control, Multi-Tenancy & The Demo Company

This is now a core project capability, not a side feature. The system must answer two questions before every retrieval, query, download, or agent action:

  1. Who is the user? Authentication establishes identity.
  2. Is this user allowed to access this resource or perform this action? Authorization makes the actual security decision.
Selected MVP Direction Build a fictional company called NileTech Solutions, plus a second small tenant called Delta Retail Group for isolation tests. Use FastAPI-issued JWTs for authentication, PostgreSQL for roles and policies, and a combined RBAC + ABAC authorization model. Generate the data with deterministic scripts so the full demo can be recreated at any time. Keycloak remains an optional later replacement for the login/identity layer, not an MVP dependency.

Permission-aware RAG

Each Qdrant chunk must be stored with access metadata. The retriever applies filters before semantic search results are returned:

{
  "company_id": "niletech",
  "document_id": "doc_981",
  "department_id": "hr",
  "country": "EG",
  "classification": "CONFIDENTIAL",
  "allowed_roles": ["hr", "company_admin"],

Security rule: unauthorized chunks never enter the LLM context window.

The ingestion pipeline is responsible for attaching this metadata to every chunk. The query pipeline receives an already-built access context from the Backend and converts it into Qdrant filters. The LLM is never asked to decide which confidential text it should hide; data that fails the policy is never retrieved.

This is the section that separates a real graduation project from "we called the ChatGPT API." Be explicit about this in your proposal and defense.

08 · Vocabulary

Chatbot vs RAG vs Agent vs Workflow vs Multi-Agent

TermWhat it actually meansReal example
ChatbotAnswers using only what the model learned in training. No live data, no actions.A generic assistant answering "what is a JWT?" from general knowledge.
RAGRetrieves real, current, private data first, then answers using that data as grounding."How many vacation days?" answered from the actual uploaded HR policy.
AI AgentCan decide which actions to take (via tool calling) and in what order, not just answer questions."Generate last month's sales report" — the agent decides to query the DB, then summarize.
Workflow AutomationA fixed, predefined sequence of steps that always runs the same way — no reasoning about what to do, just executing a pipeline.Every new contract upload automatically gets OCR'd, embedded, and tagged — same steps, every time, no decision-making.
Multi-Agent SystemSeveral specialized agents, each with a narrow job, coordinating (often via the Orchestrator) to solve something one agent alone couldn't.A "Contract Agent" extracts clauses, a "Risk Agent" flags risky terms, a "Summary Agent" writes the final comparison — each is narrow, together they're powerful.

Your project uses all five, layered: workflow automation for ingestion, RAG for grounded Q&A, tool-calling agents for actions, and (as a stretch goal) a small multi-agent setup for genuinely multi-step tasks like the contract comparison. That layering — not any single piece — is what makes it a real "AI operating system" rather than a single feature.

10 · Schedule

A Realistic 5-Month Timeline — Optimized

The straightforward version of this timeline just appends "agentic upgrades" as a new phase at the end — but that quietly extends the project past 5 months, and puts your most impressive demo material at the point where student teams are most likely to run out of time. The optimized version below folds each upgrade into the phase where it's cheapest to build, so the schedule stays at 20 weeks and nothing impressive depends on borrowed time.

  • Self-correction loop moves into Phase 4, next to the Hallucination Checker — it's a retry branch on a component you're already building, not new work.
  • Scheduled/proactive agent moves into Phase 3, next to the SQL agent — a scheduler that re-runs an existing agent on a timer is far cheaper once the agent already exists.
  • Human-approval gate moves into Phase 3 as well, right alongside the first tool that can act (email/SQL) — approval should exist from the moment anything irreversible is possible, not bolted on afterward.
  • Adaptive re-planning & multi-agent handoff stay as explicitly optional, attempted only in Phase 5 if every earlier gate closed early.
Phase 1 — Foundations (Weeks 1–4)

11 · Plain Language

The Concepts, As Analogies

EmbeddingsTurning a sentence into a set of GPS coordinates for "meaning." Two sentences that mean similar things end up as nearby coordinates, even if they don't share a single word — "vacation days" and "annual leave" land close together on this meaning-map.
Vector DatabaseA librarian who doesn't organize books alphabetically, but by what they're about. Ask "how much time off do I get," and she walks straight to the shelf about leave policy — even though your question never used the word "leave."
RAGAn open-book exam. The LLM isn't relying on memory (which can be wrong or outdated); it's handed the exact relevant pages of "the book" right before answering, and told to answer from those pages only.
Authentication vs AuthorizationAuthentication is the receptionist checking your ID at the building entrance. Authorization is every locked room inside checking whether your badge opens that specific door.
RBAC + ABACRBAC is the job title printed on the badge; ABAC is the extra context on the door — your department, branch, project, manager relationship, and whether the file is confidential.
Multi-TenancyTwo companies can rent offices in the same building and share elevators and electricity, but their keys must never open each other's offices. The infrastructure is shared; the data boundary is not.
The PlannerA dispatcher at a taxi call center. It doesn't drive anywhere itself — it just listens to the request and decides which driver (which tool/agent) is the right one to send.
Tool CallingGiving the LLM hands, not just a mouth. Without it, the model can only describe what a sales report might look like. With it, the model can actually reach out, pull the real numbers, and hand you the real report.
AgentsA junior employee with a specific job description and a specific set of tools they're allowed to use — the "Sales Agent" can query the sales DB but wouldn't know what to do with a contract, the same way an accountant wouldn't be asked to review your GitHub pull request.
LangGraph (or any orchestration graph)A flowchart that's actually alive — instead of a static diagram on a whiteboard, it's the literal code path a request follows, with real branches ("if this kind of question, go here; if that kind, go there") and the ability to loop back if a step fails.
Fine-TuningSending a generalist doctor to a two-week specialist course. They already knew general medicine (pre-training); the course reshapes their instincts specifically for one narrow specialty (your company's tone, or one classification task) without making them relearn medicine from scratch.
Hallucination CheckerAn editor who reads the journalist's draft and checks every fact against the interview transcript before it's allowed to print — not judging whether it "sounds right," but whether it's actually traceable to a source.
Scheduler / Background WorkerThe difference between an assistant who only answers when you knock on their door, and one who already left the report on your desk this morning because they know you need it every Monday.
Human Approval GateA seatbelt, not a speed limit. It doesn't slow down anything the system is confident and safe doing on its own — it only clicks in the moment right before something irreversible happens.

Reading it line by line

  • Frontend → Backend is the only path a user ever touches directly. Every other arrow in this diagram is invisible to them — except the Approval Prompt, which is the one moment the Orchestrator reaches back out to ask before acting.
  • Backend is the identity gatekeeper: it verifies the JWT and builds a trusted access context. The Authorization layer then decides what that identity may access before any database, vector, storage, cache, or agent operation executes.
  • The Authorization / Policy Enforcement layer combines tenant isolation, RBAC, ABAC, resource ACLs, and data classification. A denial stops the flow immediately and creates an audit event; an allowed decision produces narrow filters used by every downstream component.
  • PostgreSQL, Redis, Object Storage sit beside the Backend as its three kinds of memory: structured/permanent, fast/temporary, and raw-file, respectively.
  • The Scheduler/Worker is the only box that can start a request without a human — it uses Redis as its job queue and feeds into the exact same Orchestrator entry point a chat message would, which is why it required almost no new logic to add.
  • The AI Orchestrator receives a question plus an immutable, server-built permission scope. It can route and reason inside that scope, but it cannot expand the scope, change the tenant, or grant a tool permission to itself.
  • Inside the Orchestrator, the Planner is the fork in the road, but every path stays permission-aware: Qdrant receives payload filters, PostgreSQL receives tenant/RLS constraints, Object Storage receives authorized keys, and every tool checks a declared permission before it runs.
  • The LLM sits after all data-gathering steps converge — it never generates an answer from nothing; it always generates from assembled, retrieved, real context.
  • The Hallucination Checker now has two possible exits instead of one: a fail sends the request back up to the Planner (self-correction) instead of forward to the user, and a pass continues down toward execution.
  • The Human Approval Gate only activates for the subset of tool calls that write or send something irreversible — most requests (a lookup, a read-only report) skip straight past it.
  • The Evaluation Module runs off to the side, on its own schedule, continuously grading the system against test questions — this is your ongoing proof of quality, not a one-time demo trick.

Hold this one diagram in your head, and every individual technology — Qdrant, LangGraph, fine-tuning, FastAPI — stops being an isolated buzzword and becomes a labeled box with a clear job, a clear reason to exist, and a clear arrow showing who it talks to. That's the entire point of this document.

13 · Optional Stretch

Going Further, If Time Allows

The two highest-payoff agentic upgrades — the self-correction loop and the scheduled/proactive agent — are no longer "extra." They're built into the core system now: you can see them in the architecture diagram in Section 12, they have named owners in Section 09, and they're scheduled inside the mandatory weeks of Section 10. What's left here are the two upgrades that genuinely are optional — attempt them only in the Phase 5 stretch slot, and only if every earlier gate closed on time.

Plans that adapt mid-task

Your Planner currently commits to a plan up front (e.g. "retrieve doc A, retrieve doc B, then compare"). A more adaptive version re-plans after each step, based on what it just found — necessary for genuinely open-ended asks like "investigate why sales dropped last month," where the right next step depends entirely on what the first query reveals, not something you could specify in advance.

Multi-agent handoff, formalized

You already use an informal version of this in the contract-comparison scenario. Formalizing it means naming separate agents with narrow, explicit jobs that call each other directly — e.g. a Research Agent gathers information and hands it to a Writer Agent that drafts the final document — rather than one Planner quietly doing both jobs itself.

Why these two stay optional: both require the Orchestrator to hold and reason about more state across multiple turns, which is a meaningfully larger engineering lift than a retry branch or a cron trigger — exactly the kind of work that's safe to attempt only once the mandatory system is already stable and demoable.
